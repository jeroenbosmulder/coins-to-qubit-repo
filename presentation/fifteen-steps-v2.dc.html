ACT I · STEPS 1–5

A coin, honestly kept

flip · count · bet · and refuse to be sloppy

STEP 1/15 — FLIP A FAIR COINACT I · A COIN, HONESTLY KEPT

Randomness is clumpier than you think

Guess first: what does a truly random run of ten flips look like? Write it down, then flip.

Before any flip, the honest statement is a bet: heads with probability p, tails with the rest.

Flip long enough and the running fraction settles — the odds are a real, measurable property of the coin.

STEP 2/15 — THREE MYSTERY COINSACT I · A COIN, HONESTLY KEPT

Not every fifty-fifty is the same fifty-fifty

One coin is fair, one is biased, one has already made up its mind — you just do not know which side.

Flipping unmasks the biased coin fast. But the fair coin and the decided coin both start as honest 50/50 bets.

Same number, different kind of not-knowing: randomness that stays vs. a fact not yet learned.

STEP 3/15 — TWO NUMBERS PER RUNACT I · A COIN, HONESTLY KEPT

Score every run twice: odds and band width

Each run of 30 flips gets two numbers: its average p̂ and its band width. Bring back step 2's three coins and run them all, many times.

Your belief is fixed before flipping: it is the expected pair, not any single run.

Fair and biased both earn seats on one curve — (½, ½) and (0.8, 0.4). The mystery coin's belief averages to (½, 0): strictly inside.

STEP 4/15 — THE SEMICIRCLEACT I · A COIN, HONESTLY KEPT

Every definite coin lives on a circle

The band width is not free: σ = √(p(1−p)). Rearranged: (p − ½)² + σ² = ¼ — a circle, radius ½, centered on fair odds.

The chords to the ends, √p and √(1−p), always meet at a right angle (Thales). Beliefs have acquired geometry.

CLAIM № 2 & 3 · COX + MIT — FIRST SIGHTINGThe needle beneath the odds, and a state space with room off the poles — both just appeared in a coin drawing.
STEP 5/15 — MIXING BELIEFSACT I · A COIN, HONESTLY KEPT

Blends fill the half-disk — two uncertainties, one picture

Believe coin q₁ with some weight, q₂ with the rest: both scores average, so your belief slides along the chord.

All chords together fill the interior: the upper half-disk is the space of every honest belief.

Rim = randomness that stays. Depth inside = ignorance that flips can cure. The mystery coin sits at the center of the floor.

ACT II · STEPS 6–8

Nature's second example: light

what is polarization · partly polarized = a mixed belief · the circle completes

STEP 6/15 · I — WHAT IS POLARIZATION?ACT II · NATURE'S SECOND EXAMPLE

Polarization is a sideways wiggle — two sizes, one lock

Light is a wave that wiggles sideways as it flies: an H wiggle of size a and a V wiggle of size b, both at once, locked by a² + b² = 1.

That pair (a, b) is all polarization means. A sheet keeps only the wiggle along its slots: fraction a² of the brightness passes (Malus 1808).

Drag β to repaint the split — and note the flip switch. Park it: that innocent switch is the hero of Act IV.

STEP 6/15 · II — A COIN MADE OF LIGHTACT II · NATURE'S SECOND EXAMPLE

Dim the lamp: brightness becomes chance

Light arrives in indivisible grains — photons. A grain cannot 30%-pass a sheet: it passes whole or not at all.

So the brightness fraction a² must be each photon's chance. Brightness was probability in disguise.

A beam, a sheet, a click: step 1's coin, made of light. The wave is square-roots-of-odds, flying through the room.

CLAIM № 2 · COX — CAUGHT IN THE WILDCox's little quantities, found flying through the room: the beam is a sum of (a, b) — squared only at the detector.
STEP 6/15 · III — MANY BEAMS, ONE QUESTIONACT II · NATURE'S SECOND EXAMPLE

Lamplight redraws the coin's semicircle

Keep the sheet fixed — one question, never turned. Send beams of every tilt through it, 30 photons per run, and score each run like a coin: average and band width.

The points land on the coin's semicircle — never-passes and always-passes at the ends, the 45° beam on top.

Nature just redrew our drawing, in glass. Time to wonder about step 4's little needle…

STEP 7/15 · I — THE SAME DISK, IN GLASSACT II · NATURE'S SECOND EXAMPLE

Partly polarized light is a mixed belief

Now turn the sheet too: prepare at β, ask at θ, odds cos²(θ−β). Fully polarized beams walk the rim of the disk — pure states, in glass.

Drag inward: partly polarized light — exactly a blend of pure beams, a mixed belief; the unpolarized bulb is the center.

Degree of polarization (Stokes 1852) = step 5's confidence score. And step 4's needle? The pair (a, b) — flying through the room.

STEP 7/15 · II — THE SAME DISK, IN GLASSACT II · NATURE'S SECOND EXAMPLE

Nature shows a state our drawing cannot seat

Drag past the axis: the 135° beam — a perfectly honest, fully polarized beam that matches the fair coin's flip odds exactly.

Yet behind a 45° sheet it goes dark, where the 45° beam sails through. Two states, same odds — measurably different.

Our half-disk has one seat for fifty-fifty on the rim — already taken. We must have thrown a detail away.

STEP 8/15 — THE MIRROR TWINSACT II · NATURE'S SECOND EXAMPLE

Orientation was the lost detail — the circle completes

Every state carries a triangle over the T–H diameter. Its mirror twin below has identical side lengths — and opposite turning.

Our scores kept lengths and dropped orientation: a sign. Keeping it completes the half-circle to the full Bernoulli circle.

The fair coin's lower twin is the anti-coin — the 135° beam, named in coin language. Coins alone never showed the lower half: linear light hands us the full circle.

ACT III · STEPS 9–11

Questions, signs, amplitudes

every diameter asks · the flip is symmetric · Thales pays out

STEP 9/15 — MEASURING IS ASKINGACT III · QUESTIONS, SIGNS, AMPLITUDES

Every diameter is a question: ‘which end?’

You may never ask a state where are you? — only pick a diameter and ask which end?

The answer odds are drawn in the picture: the squared chord to the opposite end. Thales + Pythagoras make them sum to 1.

The horizontal diameter is the coin flip. Tilted diameters are new questions — and a rotated polarizer sheet is one, in glass.

STEP 10/15 — WHY THE SIGN HIDESACT III · QUESTIONS, SIGNS, AMPLITUDES

The flip is blind by symmetry — the tilt is not

The flip's two endpoints sit on the mirror line, so a state and its twin keep identical flip odds — forever.

Drag the diameter: flat → the twins answer alike; tilted → they split. The 45°-sheet test of step 7, as pure geometry.

Classical probability is what owning only the flat question feels like. A rotating sheet is owning all of them.

STEP 11/15 — THE VIEW FROM THE STATEACT III · QUESTIONS, SIGNS, AMPLITUDES

The amplitudes were the light all along

Thales hands every state two chords, √p and √(1−p): squared, they are the answer odds. Born's rule is triangle geometry.

The chord pair (a, b) lives on a unit circle; squaring sends α → 2α — the lab's angle-doubling, derived — and one full lap flips (a, b) to (−a, −b): the same state.

For light this pair is hardware: the H and V wiggles. Malus 1808 = the squared-chord rule, measured with a crystal and a sunset.

CLAIM № 2 · COX — REDEEMEDThe little quantities have their official name — amplitudes — computed first, added, squared last.
ACT IV · STEPS 12–13

Circular light — the ball, already named

circular light · the delay dial · Poincaré 1892

STEP 12/15 — THE STATE THAT ISN'T THEREACT IV · CIRCULAR LIGHT — DISK TO BALL

A pure state the whole disk cannot seat

Unlock step 6's switch into a timing dial δ: 0° a line, 180° the twin's line, 90° at equal wiggles — a circle. The beam corkscrews.

Watch the meter curve: at the circle it goes flat — every sheet reads 50%, ‘dead center, same as the bulb’.

Yet a passive quarter-wave plate revives it, losslessly, to a pure beam. Blends cannot be un-blended — so this pure state has no seat on the disk.

STEP 13/15 — THE DELAY DIAL: DISK TO BALLACT IV · CIRCULAR LIGHT — DISK TO BALL

The switch ripens into a dial — and the disk into a ball

On every diameter, stand a wheel: φ = 0 is the state, 180° its twin, and ±90° the corkscrew beams the disk had no seat for.

The circle inflates into a sphere; blends fill the ball; ignorance sits at the center. And it already had a name: Poincaré 1892, Bloch 1946.

Certainty on one diameter forces 50/50 on every perpendicular one: uncertainty is the roundness of the ball.

ACT V · STEPS 14–15

interference in your hands · the smallest quantum computation

STEP 14/15 — THE BALL, IN YOUR HANDSACT V · ONE PHOTON AT A TIME

Light resurrected by an obstacle

Cross two sheets: darkness. Slide a third between them at 45°: light returns — an added obstacle made more light.

The middle sheet asks the 45° question and re-prepares its answer; two opposite-sign contributions stop cancelling. Interference, on bright classical light.

And the full dictionary: H/V, the ±45° coin and anti-coin, left/right circular — three famous pairs, three perpendicular diameters of your ball.

STEP 15/15 — ONE PHOTON AT A TIMEACT V · ONE PHOTON AT A TIME

The smallest quantum computation there is

Two routes are one qubit: half-mirrors are quarter-turns; path length turns the delay dial φ.

Fringes cos²(φ/2) from single clicks — while the mystery-mixture source (one route, unknown which) stays flat forever.

No ‘one route’ story survives: the amplitudes travel with each particle. Prepare · turn · read — the smallest quantum computation.

EPILOGUE

The ball in the wild

The ball grew out of honest statistics — coins, then lamplight — long before quantum theory named it. The wonder is what happens as the lamp dims: one indivisible particle carries the whole ball. Electron spins, photon polarization, two levels of an atom — each one is this ball, and its rotations are the gates of a quantum computer.
